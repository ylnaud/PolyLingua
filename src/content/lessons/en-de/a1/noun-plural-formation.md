English handles plurals with one trick: add -s. German is not that simple — every noun
belongs to one of five plural patterns, and the pattern is tied to the individual word,
not to a spelling rule you can apply on sight. The good news: once you learn the five
patterns, you can recognize which one is likely at a glance, even if you cannot always
predict it with 100% certainty.

## The one rule that never changes: plural is always "die"

Before the five patterns, one simplification makes all of this easier: no matter what
article a noun had in the singular — der, die, or das — the plural article is **always
die**.

```
der Tisch  →  die Tische
das Kind   →  die Kinder
die Frau   →  die Frauen
```

You never have to guess the plural article. You only have to learn the plural *ending*.

## The five plural patterns

| Pattern | What happens | Examples |
| ------- | ------------- | -------- |
| No ending (sometimes + umlaut) | The word does not change, or only gets an umlaut | der Lehrer → die Lehrer; der Vater → die Väter |
| -e (often + umlaut) | Add -e, sometimes with an umlaut on the vowel | der Tisch → die Tische; der Stuhl → die Stühle |
| -er (+ umlaut when possible) | Add -er, and add an umlaut whenever the vowel can take one | das Kind → die Kinder; das Buch → die Bücher |
| -(e)n | Add -n or -en, never with an umlaut | die Frau → die Frauen; die Lampe → die Lampen |
| -s | Add -s — typical of loanwords from other languages | das Auto → die Autos; das Hotel → die Hotels |

## Tendencies that help you guess

These are not fixed rules, but they raise your odds of guessing correctly on a new word:

- **Most feminine nouns** take -(e)n: die Frau → die Frauen, die Zeitung → die Zeitungen.
  This is the most reliable of the five patterns.
- **Many neuter nouns** take -er with an umlaut: das Kind → die Kinder, das Buch → die
  Bücher, das Haus → die Häuser.
- **Masculine nouns** are the least predictable — they can take -e (der Tisch → die
  Tische), no ending (der Lehrer → die Lehrer), or -er (der Mann → die Männer).
- **Loanwords** (words borrowed from English, French, and other languages) almost always
  take -s: das Auto, das Hotel, das Handy → die Handys.

## Why the umlaut matters

An umlaut (the two dots over ä, ö, ü) changes the pronunciation of the vowel, and in
plurals it often signals the plural form all by itself, even without an ending:

```
der Vater  →  die Väter   (no ending added, just the umlaut)
der Bruder →  die Brüder  (no ending added, just the umlaut)
die Mutter →  die Mütter  (no ending added, just the umlaut)
```

Not every noun that could take an umlaut does — this is another reason plurals have to
be memorized individually rather than derived from a rule.

## The learning strategy that actually works

Just like you learn a noun together with its article (der Tisch, not just "Tisch"), learn
it together with its plural. Most German dictionaries and vocabulary lists already show
this for you, in a shorthand like "der Tisch, -e" (meaning the plural is "die Tische") or
"das Kind, -er" (meaning "die Kinder"). Get used to reading that shorthand — it will save
you from having to guess later.
