Every verb conjugation you learn in German is built around six "slots" — the six personal
pronouns. Get comfortable with them now, because you will see this exact six-way split
attached to nearly every verb for the rest of your German studies.

## The six pronouns

| German     | English        | Notes                                    |
| ----------- | --------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| ich         | I               | always lowercase                          |
| du          | you (informal)  | one friend, child, family member          |
| er / sie / es | he / she / it | matches the noun's grammatical gender     |
| wir         | we               | first-person plural                        |
| ihr         | you all (informal) | more than one friend                   |
| sie / Sie   | they / you (formal) | lowercase = they, capitalized = formal you |

## The three ways to say "you"

This is the part that has no direct English equivalent, and it is worth slowing down for:

- **du** — one person you know well: a friend, a family member, a child, a fellow
  student. Using du with the wrong person can sound overly familiar or even rude.
- **ihr** — more than one person, all of whom you would individually address with du.
  Think of it as the plural of du.
- **Sie** — anyone you address formally: strangers, service staff, your boss, an older
  person you just met. Sie is always capitalized, even mid-sentence, which is what
  visually separates it from lowercase "sie" (she/they).

As a beginner, a safe default is to use **Sie** with any adult stranger and switch to
**du** only once that person invites you to ("Wir können uns duzen" — we can use du with
each other), or when you are speaking with someone clearly younger than you or in a casual
setting like among students.

## Er, sie, es: gender follows the noun, not biology

German assigns er, sie, or es to a noun based on its grammatical gender — der/die/das —
not on whether the thing itself has a biological gender:

- der Tisch (the table) → **er** ist groß. (It is big.)
- die Lampe (the lamp) → **sie** ist neu. (It is new.)
- das Auto (the car) → **es** ist schnell. (It is fast.)

This can feel strange at first — a table is "he" and a lamp is "she" — but it simply
follows the article the noun already has, which you learn together with each new word.

## One word, three meanings: sie / sie / Sie

German recycles the spelling "sie" for three different jobs. Context and capitalization
tell them apart:

1. **sie** (she) — Sie ist meine Schwester. (She is my sister.)
2. **sie** (they) — Sie sind meine Freunde. (They are my friends.)
3. **Sie** (formal you, always capitalized) — Sind Sie Frau Müller? (Are you Ms. Müller?)

When reading, capitalization mid-sentence is your clue that you are looking at formal
"you," not "she" or "they."
