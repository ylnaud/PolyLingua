You already know how to conjugate sein and haben. This lesson puts them to work in the
kind of everyday sentences you will actually say in your first real conversations — and
warns you about a trap that catches almost every English speaker at least once.

## Sein for identity and states

Use **sein** whenever you are stating who or what someone/something is, or describing a
condition with an adjective:

- Ich bin Student. — I am a student.
- Ich bin müde. — I am tired.
- Bist du aus Deutschland? — Are you from Germany?
- Er ist nett. — He is nice.

This matches English fairly closely: "to be" + noun, or "to be" + adjective.

## Haben for possession — and for feelings English does not expect

Use **haben** for possession, the way English uses "have":

- Ich habe einen Hund. — I have a dog.
- Wir haben Zeit. — We have time.
- Hast du ein Auto? — Do you have a car?

So far, so predictable. But German also uses haben for a small group of feelings and
states that English expresses with "to be" — and these have to be memorized as fixed
phrases, because there is no grammatical rule that predicts them:

| German (with haben) | Literal translation | English meaning |
| --------------------- | --------------------- | ----------------- |
| Ich habe Hunger.     | I have hunger        | I am hungry.      |
| Ich habe Durst.      | I have thirst         | I am thirsty.     |
| Ich habe Angst.      | I have fear            | I am afraid.      |
| Ich habe Recht.      | I have right(ness)   | I am right.       |

A very common beginner mistake is saying *Ich bin hungrig* instead of *Ich habe Hunger*.
Ich bin hungrig is not wrong exactly, but Ich habe Hunger is what a native speaker
actually reaches for in conversation — so it is worth training your instinct now.

## Asking how someone is

Interestingly, the standard greeting "How are you?" uses neither sein nor haben directly
— it uses the verb **gehen** (to go):

- Wie geht es dir? — How are you? (informal)
- Wie geht es Ihnen? — How are you? (formal)
- Mir geht es gut. — I am doing well. (literally: "It goes well for me")

You do not need to conjugate gehen fully yet — just recognize and memorize this phrase as
a fixed expression, the same way you memorized the haben idioms above.

## Putting it together: a short exchange

```
A: Hallo! Wie geht es dir?          Hello! How are you?
B: Mir geht es gut, danke. Und dir?  I am doing well, thanks. And you?
A: Auch gut. Hast du Hunger?         Also good. Are you hungry?
B: Ja, ich habe Hunger!              Yes, I am hungry!
```

Notice how naturally sein, haben, and the gehen-greeting mix together in a single short
conversation — this is exactly the kind of exchange you will have constantly as a
beginner, so it is worth practicing out loud.
