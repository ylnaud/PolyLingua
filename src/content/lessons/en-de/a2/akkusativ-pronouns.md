You already know that German articles change when something is what you have, see, or
buy (accusative: der → den, mein → meinen). Personal pronouns change too when they play
that role — and the good news is most of them already look familiar.

## Normal form vs. accusative (when it is what you see/have)

| Nominative | Accusative | Change...      |
| ---------- | ---------- | --------------- |
| ich        | mich       | adds -ch        |
| du         | dich       | adds -ch        |
| er         | ihn        | completely      |
| sie (she)  | sie        | no change       |
| es         | es         | no change       |
| wir        | uns        | completely      |
| ihr        | euch       | completely      |
| sie (they) | sie        | no change       |
| Sie        | Sie        | no change       |

```
Ich liebe dich.        Sie ruft mich an.        Wir besuchen sie.
```

## The one you actually need to memorize from scratch: ihn

`er` → `ihn` is the least intuitive change on this list, because it does not resemble
the original form at all. Every other pronoun either adds something small to a form
you already know (ich→mich, du→dich) or simply does not change (sie, es, Sie). Put
your memorization effort there.

## Common verbs that take an accusative pronoun

These pronouns show up constantly after verbs where someone sees, has, or looks for
something or someone:

```
sehen (to see)        Ich sehe ihn.
lieben (to love)      Ich liebe dich.
kennen (to know)      Kennst du sie?
besuchen (to visit)   Wir besuchen sie.
fragen (to ask)       Ich frage dich.
```

## How to practice

Take 5 simple sentences with names ("Ich sehe Anna", "Ich rufe Peter an") and swap the
name for its accusative pronoun ("Ich sehe sie", "Ich rufe ihn an"). It is the same
exercise you already did with articles, applied to people.
