Going to the doctor in another language is scary, but it comes down to just a few
phrases: saying how you are, where it hurts, and since when.

## How you're doing, in general

```
Ich bin krank.                I am sick.
Ich fühle mich nicht gut.     I don't feel well.
```

`sich fühlen` is reflexive: `ich fühle **mich**`, `du fühlst **dich**`.

## Symptoms go with haben

Just like `Hunger` or `Durst`: **with haben and no article**.

```
Ich habe Kopfschmerzen.       I have a headache.
Ich habe Bauchschmerzen.      I have a stomach ache.
Ich habe Fieber.              I have a fever.
```

Notice how pain words are built by joining the body part + `-schmerzen`:
`Kopf` + `schmerzen`, `Bauch` + `schmerzen`, `Rücken` + `schmerzen`.

## The other way: wehtun

```
Mir tut der Rücken weh.       My back hurts.
Mir tut der Hals weh.         My throat hurts.
```

The structure is the person in `mir/dir/ihm` (Dativ = "to whom") with the body part
as the subject — literally "to me hurts the back." There's nothing new to learn here
grammatically, just the verb `wehtun` itself.

## Booking an appointment and answering the doctor

```
Ich brauche einen Termin.              I need an appointment.
Haben Sie heute einen Termin frei?     Do you have an appointment free today?

— Seit wann haben Sie Schmerzen?       Since when have you had pain?
— Seit drei Tagen.                     For three days.
```

And when you leave, you'll hear **`Gute Besserung!`** (get well soon).

## How to practice

Pick three body parts and say both forms out loud for each: `Ich habe
Rückenschmerzen` and `Mir tut der Rücken weh`. They're interchangeable, and having
both gives you a way out no matter which one comes to mind first.
