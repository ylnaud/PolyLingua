Talking about your free time is one of the most natural conversations you can have in
German — it comes up constantly with new friends, classmates, and coworkers. Here is
the essential vocabulary, plus the two small grammar tools that make it work.

## Common activities

| German                  | English                     |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------- |
| lesen                    | to read                      |
| Sport machen             | to do sports, to exercise    |
| schwimmen                | to swim                      |
| kochen                   | to cook                      |
| Musik hören              | to listen to music           |
| ins Kino gehen           | to go to the movies          |
| sich mit Freunden treffen | to meet up with friends       |
| wandern                  | to hike                      |
| malen                    | to paint                     |

## Saying what you like with "gern"

German does not have a direct verb like "to like" for activities the way English
does — instead, you conjugate the activity's own verb normally and add the small word
**gern** right after it:

```
Ich lese gern.              (I like reading.)
Ich koche nicht gern.       (I don't like cooking.)
Ich schwimme sehr gern.     (I like swimming very much.)
Ich höre gern Musik.        (I like listening to music.)
```

"gern" always sits directly after the conjugated verb, even when an object like
"Musik" follows. Adding "sehr" (very) before it, or "nicht" (not) before it, adjusts
how much you like — or dislike — the activity.

## Asking about someone else's free time

```
Was machst du in deiner Freizeit?    (What do you do in your free time?)
Hast du ein Hobby?                    (Do you have a hobby?)
Treibst du Sport?                     (Do you play any sports?)
```

## The all-purpose verb: machen

"machen" (to do/make) combines with a huge number of nouns to form activity phrases:
Sport machen, Musik machen, eine Reise machen (to take a trip), eine Pause machen (to
take a break). Once you know this pattern, you can improvise new vocabulary with
confidence instead of needing a separate verb for every activity.

## A reflexive bonus: treffen

If you have covered reflexive verbs already, "sich mit Freunden treffen" (to meet up
with friends) is a great everyday example: the reflexive pronoun changes with the
subject exactly like "sich freuen" or "sich waschen" — Ich treffe mich mit Freunden,
Wir treffen uns mit Freunden.
