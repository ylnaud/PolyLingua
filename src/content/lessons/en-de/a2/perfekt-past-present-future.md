To talk about your day, you don't need every German verb tense. You need **one for the
past** and **the present tense for the future**. That's it.

## The past: always Perfekt

Forget about the Präteritum for now. In speech, almost nobody says `Ich ging ins Kino`
— people say:

```
Ich bin ins Kino gegangen.        I went to the movies.
Ich habe viel gearbeitet.         I worked a lot.
Ich habe einen Freund getroffen.  I met up with a friend.
```

The structure is always the same: **auxiliary in second position, participle at the
end.**

## The only real question: haben or sein?

```
sein   → verbs of motion or change of state:  gehen, kommen, fahren
haben  → everything else:                     arbeiten, machen, essen, treffen
```

```
Ich bin nach Hause gegangen.     (motion → sein)
Ich habe viel gegessen.          (everything else → haben)
```

The large majority of verbs take `haben`. If you're unsure, `haben` is the safer bet.

## The future: it doesn't really exist (for everyday talk)

To talk about tomorrow, you don't need a future tense at all. You use the **present
tense** and add when:

```
Morgen arbeite ich.               Tomorrow I work.
Morgen gehe ich einkaufen.        Tomorrow I'm going shopping.
Nächste Woche habe ich frei.      Next week I have off.
```

Watch the word order: if the sentence starts with `Morgen`, the verb goes
**immediately after it** — `Morgen arbeite ich`, not _"Morgen ich arbeite"_. The verb
always stays in second position.

## How to practice

Every night, tell your day in three Perfekt sentences and tomorrow's plan in one
present-tense sentence. That's four sentences a day, always the same structure. In two
weeks you'll be building the past without thinking about the rule.
