Indirect questions are a more polite and natural way to ask something, especially in
formal contexts — and they follow the same "verb at the end" logic you already know
from subordinate clauses.

## Two paths depending on the type of question

**Yes/no questions** → introduced with "ob"

```
Direct:    Kommt sie heute?
Indirect:  Ich weiß nicht, ob sie heute kommt.
```

**Questions with a question word (W-question)** → the same question word is reused

```
Direct:    Wo wohnst du?
Indirect:  Ich möchte wissen, wo du wohnst.
```

Notice what stays the same and what changes: the question word itself (wo, wann,
was...) never changes — only the position of the verb does.

## The rule that never changes: verb at the end

```
Ich frage mich,    ob    sie    heute    kommt.
Können Sie mir sagen,    wann    der Zug    ankommt?
```

Whether the indirect question uses "ob" or a question word, the conjugated verb
always ends up at the very end of its clause — exactly like weil, dass, and obwohl.

## Useful introductory phrases

```
Ich weiß nicht, ob...          (I don't know whether...)
Ich frage mich, ob/wann/wo...  (I wonder whether/when/where...)
Können Sie mir sagen, ob...    (Could you tell me whether...?)
Ich möchte wissen, was...      (I would like to know what...)
```

Memorize these frames as complete building blocks — you can drop almost any yes/no
question after "ob" or any W-question after its own question word, and the word
order will already be correct.

## Why it is worth mastering

Asking a stranger something directly ("Ist der Zug pünktlich?") can sound blunt in
certain formal situations. Wrapping the question in an indirect structure
("Können Sie mir sagen, ob der Zug pünktlich ist?") softens the tone — a tool that
polite German speakers use constantly, in shops, offices, and on the phone.
