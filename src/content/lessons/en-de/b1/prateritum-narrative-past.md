You already know the Perfekt for talking about the past. Now meet its more formal
sibling: the **Präteritum**, the tense that dominates books, news reports, and
written narration.

## When each one is used

| Perfekt                 | Präteritum                          |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------ |
| Everyday conversation    | Written texts, news, stories         |
| "Ich habe gegessen"      | "Ich aß"                             |

If you are speaking out loud about something that happened, the Perfekt is your
default. If you are reading (or writing) a story, article, or novel, expect the
Präteritum instead.

## The exception that breaks the rule

Even though the Perfekt dominates spoken German, a handful of verbs almost always
show up in the Präteritum even in conversation:

```
ich war (sein)       ich hatte (haben)       ich konnte (können)
```

Saying "ich bin gewesen" instead of "ich war" is grammatically correct but sounds
unnecessarily long-winded — German speakers default to the short form here.

## Forming the Präteritum: two paths

**Weak (regular) verbs**: stem + -te

| Pronoun    | machen    |
| ---------- | --------- |
| ich        | machte    |
| du         | machtest  |
| er/sie/es  | machte    |
| wir        | machten   |

**Strong (irregular) verbs**: the stem vowel changes, and ich/er-sie-es get no ending

```
gehen → ging       sehen → sah       kommen → kam
```

There is no shortcut for strong verbs — their Präteritum forms simply have to be
learned individually, the same way English speakers learn "go → went" rather than
deriving it from a rule.

## The trick for recognizing it while reading

When you read a story or news article in German and spot forms like "er ging", "sie
sah", "wir hatten", you now know you are looking at the Präteritum — the default
narrative tense of written German.
