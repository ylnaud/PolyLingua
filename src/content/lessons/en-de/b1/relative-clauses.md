You already know der/die/das as articles. Now you will learn to reuse them — same
look, different job — to connect two sentences into one.

## The logic: two sentences become one

```
Der Mann ist mein Lehrer. + Der Mann steht dort.
→ Der Mann, der dort steht, ist mein Lehrer.
```

Instead of repeating "der Mann" twice, German replaces the second mention with a
relative pronoun and folds the whole thing into a single, connected sentence — much
like English "the man **who** is standing there".

## Table of relative pronouns

| Case      | maskulin | feminin | neutral | Plural |
| --------- | -------- | ------- | ------- | ------ |
| Nominativ | der      | die     | das     | die    |
| Akkusativ | den      | die     | das     | die    |
| Dativ     | dem      | der     | dem     | denen  |

This is almost exactly the same table you already know from the definite articles —
with one exception: Dativ Plural is **denen**, not the article "den".

## Two steps to choose the right pronoun

1. **Gender/number**: look at the noun you are describing (der Mann → masculine
   singular → der/den/dem)
2. **Case**: look at what function the pronoun performs INSIDE the relative clause
   (subject → Nominativ, direct object → Akkusativ, etc.)

```
Der Mann, der dort steht...       (der = subject of "steht" → Nominativ)
Der Mann, den ich sehe...          (den = object of "sehe" → Akkusativ)
Der Mann, dem ich helfe...         (dem = "helfen" requires Dativ)
```

Notice that the gender comes from the noun outside the clause, but the case comes
entirely from what the pronoun is doing inside its own clause — the two decisions are
independent of each other.

## The verb: always at the end

Like any subordinate clause, the conjugated verb goes to the end: "der Mann, der dort
**steht**" — this pattern should already feel familiar from weil/dass/obwohl.
