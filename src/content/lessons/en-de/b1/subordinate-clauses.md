Up to now your German sentences have probably been short and direct. At this level you
start **connecting ideas**: cause, consequence, contrast, condition. That is what
separates an intermediate speaker from a beginner — and it all hinges on one habit:
knowing when the verb has to move.

## The golden rule: the verb sits down at the end

When you use a subordinating conjunction, the conjugated verb leaves its usual
comfortable "second position" and walks all the way to the **end** of its clause:

```
..., weil ich    heute    keine    Zeit    habe.
                                            ↑ the verb arrives last
```

This is not optional style — it is the single most important word-order rule in
German subordinate clauses, and it applies every time, no exceptions.

## The conjunctions you need to master

| Conjunction | Meaning     | Example                         |
| ----------- | ----------- | -------------------------------- |
| weil        | because     | ...weil ich müde bin.           |
| dass        | that        | Ich glaube, dass er recht hat.  |
| obwohl      | although    | ...obwohl es kalt ist.          |
| wenn        | if / when   | Wenn du Zeit hast, ruf mich an. |

## weil vs. denn: a trap for English speakers

Both mean "because", but only one is subordinating:

```
Ich bleibe zu Hause, weil ich krank bin.   (verb at the END: bin)
Ich bleibe zu Hause, denn ich bin krank.   (verb stays in normal position: bin ist zweit)
```

'denn' is a coordinating conjunction, like 'und' or 'aber' — it links two independent
clauses without touching word order. 'weil' is subordinating and always pushes the
verb to the end. Both are correct German; only the word order differs.

## The "mirror sentence" trick

When the subordinate clause comes **first**, the main clause flips: the main verb
jumps to the first position right after the comma.

```
Wenn es regnet,    bleibe    ich    zu Hause.
(subordinate clause)  (verb)   (subject)
```

Think of the whole subordinate clause as occupying "slot one" of the sentence — German
main clauses always want the verb in slot two, so if the subordinate clause fills slot
one, the verb has to come immediately after it.

## Try it yourself

Practice by writing three reasons why you are learning German, each with "weil" — you
will find the verb-at-the-end pattern becomes automatic surprisingly fast once you
have drilled it a few times out loud.
