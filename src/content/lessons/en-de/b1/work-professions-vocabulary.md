Talking about your job is one of the first things you do in any new conversation. This
lesson gives you the vocabulary — and one grammar rule that trips up almost every
English speaker learning German.

## The rule: no article before a profession

In English you always say "I am **A** teacher." German does the opposite: after
**sein** (to be) or **werden** (to become), professions drop the article entirely.

```
Ich bin Lehrer.        (I am a teacher — NOT "ein Lehrer")
Ich bin Ärztin.         (I am a doctor)
Er wird Ingenieur.      (He is becoming an engineer)
```

Think of it less like naming an object and more like naming a category you belong to
— similar to how English says "she is president" (no article) rather than "she is a
president" for a unique role.

## When the article comes back

As soon as you add a describing adjective, the noun phrase needs its normal article
again, adjective ending and all:

```
Ich bin ein guter Lehrer.   (I am a good teacher)
Sie ist eine erfahrene Ärztin.  (She is an experienced doctor)
```

## Forming the feminine: add -in

Almost every profession noun forms its feminine version by adding **-in** to the
masculine noun:

| Masculine       | Feminine           |
| ---------------- | -------------------- |
| der Lehrer        | die Lehrerin          |
| der Arzt           | die Ärztin (note the umlaut!) |
| der Ingenieur      | die Ingenieurin        |
| der Verkäufer      | die Verkäuferin         |

For the plural of the feminine form, double the n and add -en: die Lehrerin → die
Lehrerinnen.

## Talking about your job

Two everyday patterns cover almost every situation:

```
Was sind Sie von Beruf?     (What is your profession? — polite)
Ich arbeite als Ingenieurin bei einer Firma.
(I work as an engineer at a company.)
```

"arbeiten als" (to work as) behaves the same way as sein + profession — no article
after "als" either: Ich arbeite als Lehrer, not "als ein Lehrer".
