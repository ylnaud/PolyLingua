At B2 level, you should be able to follow a news broadcast or read a newspaper article
about economics and politics without stopping every third word. German economy and society
vocabulary looks intimidating at first because so many terms are long compound nouns — but
those compounds are built from simple pieces once you learn to break them apart.

## The compound-noun pattern

Two suffixes do most of the heavy lifting for turning adjectives into abstract nouns:

- **-heit** and **-keit**: gleich (equal) → die Gleichheit (equality); frei (free) → die
  Freiheit (freedom); möglich (possible) → die Möglichkeit (possibility).
- **-igkeit**: combines with an already-compound adjective, like arbeitslos (unemployed,
  itself Arbeit + los) → die Arbeitslosigkeit (unemployment).

Once you recognize these patterns, you can often guess the meaning of a new compound
instead of memorizing it cold.

## A note on false friends

Be careful with **die Steuer** — it means "tax," not "the steering wheel" (that's "das
Steuer" in a car, a completely different, unrelated meaning that only shares the spelling).
Context almost always disambiguates instantly: "die Steuer erhöhen" (raise taxes) versus
"das Steuer drehen" (turn the wheel).

## Using this vocabulary in a sentence

Try building full sentences instead of memorizing isolated words — it's how this vocabulary
sticks: *Die Arbeitslosigkeit ist letztes Jahr gesunken, aber die Inflation ist gestiegen.*
(Unemployment fell last year, but inflation rose.) Notice how naturally this pairs with the
advanced connectors ("aber") and passive constructions you've already studied at B2 — this
is exactly the kind of sentence you'll meet in real German news articles.
