In an active sentence, the subject does the action: *Peter öffnet das Fenster* (Peter opens
the window). In a passive sentence, the focus shifts to what is happening to something or
someone — the person doing it becomes optional, or disappears entirely: *Das Fenster wird
geöffnet* (The window is being opened). German uses the passive constantly, especially in
news, instructions, and formal writing, so recognizing and producing it is a key B2 skill.

## Building the present-tense passive

The formula is simple: **conjugated werden + Partizip II (at the end of the clause)**.

| Subject | werden | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ich | werde | Ich werde gefragt. (I am asked.) |
| du | wirst | Du wirst eingeladen. (You are invited.) |
| er/sie/es | wird | Der Brief wird geschrieben. (The letter is written.) |
| wir | werden | Wir werden informiert. (We are informed.) |
| ihr | werdet | Ihr werdet gerufen. (You all are called.) |
| sie/Sie | werden | Sie werden gesehen. (They are seen.) |

Note that this is the *same* werden you already know as "to become" — only the meaning shifts
depending on whether a participle follows it.

## Building the passive in the simple past (Präteritum)

Swap the present-tense werden for its Präteritum form, and keep the participle at the end:

| Subject | werden (Präteritum) | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ich | wurde | Ich wurde gefragt. (I was asked.) |
| du | wurdest | Du wurdest eingeladen. (You were invited.) |
| er/sie/es | wurde | Der Brief wurde geschrieben. (The letter was written.) |
| wir | wurden | Wir wurden informiert. (We were informed.) |
| ihr | wurdet | Ihr wurdet gerufen. (You all were called.) |
| sie/Sie | wurden | Sie wurden gesehen. (They were seen.) |

This is the tense you will meet most often in narratives, news reports, and history texts:
*Die Mauer wurde 1989 geöffnet.* (The Wall was opened in 1989.)

## Naming the agent: von vs. durch

Passive sentences often drop the "doer" entirely, because the point of the passive is to
de-emphasize it. But when you do want to mention who or what caused the action, German uses
two different prepositions depending on what kind of agent it is:

- **von + Dativ** — a person, animal, or organization actively doing the action:
  *Das Fenster wird von Peter geöffnet.* (The window is opened by Peter.)
- **durch + Akkusativ** — an impersonal cause, means, or intermediary force:
  *Das Dorf wurde durch den Sturm zerstört.* (The village was destroyed by the storm.)

A useful test: if you could ask "wer?" (who?) and get a living answer, use von; if the answer
is a thing, event, or mechanism, use durch.

## Passive with modal verbs

To combine a modal verb (müssen, können, sollen, dürfen, wollen) with the passive, the
sentence ends with **Partizip II + werden** as a unit — this pair is called the passive
infinitive:

- *Das Auto muss repariert werden.* (The car must be repaired.)
- *Die Aufgabe kann heute nicht gemacht werden.* (The task cannot be done today.)
- *Der Vertrag soll morgen unterschrieben werden.* (The contract is supposed to be signed
  tomorrow.)

The modal verb takes the normal position-2 slot and carries the person ending; everything else
piles up at the end of the clause, participle before werden.

## Why German uses the passive so much

English speakers are often taught to avoid the passive voice in writing, but German treats it
as completely normal and even preferred in impersonal, technical, or bureaucratic contexts —
instructions, recipes, laws, and news reports lean on it heavily precisely because it lets the
writer omit who is responsible and focus on the process itself: *Hier wird nicht geraucht.*
(No smoking here. — literally: "Here is not smoked.")
