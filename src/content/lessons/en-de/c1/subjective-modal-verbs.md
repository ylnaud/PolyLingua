Every German modal verb — müssen, können, dürfen, sollen, wollen, mögen — has two completely
different jobs. The one you learned first is **objective**: obligation, ability, permission.
The second, at C1, is **subjective**: it tells you how *certain* the speaker is about a claim,
or where that claim comes from. Same verb, same conjugation — but a totally different meaning.

## Objective vs. subjective: the same sentence, two readings

> **Er muss jetzt zu Hause sein.**

- **Objective**: He has to be home now (an obligation — someone requires it).
- **Subjective**: He must be home now (a conclusion — I'm nearly certain, based on evidence).

Context almost always makes the intended reading obvious, but recognizing that both exist is
the first step to using subjective modals confidently.

## The certainty scale

Subjective modals sit on a scale from near-fact down to open doubt:

| Modal | Strength | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **müssen** | very strong | logical near-certainty, from evidence | Er muss zu Hause sein. (He must be home.) |
| **dürfte** | confident | a strong, educated guess | Er dürfte zu Hause sein. (He is probably home.) |
| **sollte** | confident | a strong guess (near-synonym of dürfte here) | Er sollte zu Hause sein. (He should be home.) |
| **können/könnte** | possible | a real possibility, not confirmed | Er könnte zu Hause sein. (He might be home.) |
| **mag** | weak concession | "it may be, but..." | Er mag Recht haben, aber... (He may be right, but...) |

## sollen vs. wollen: whose claim is it?

These two are easy to confuse but point in opposite directions:

- **sollen** = *other people* say this about the subject (hearsay, the speaker has no personal
  proof): *Sie soll sehr reich sein.* (She is said to be very rich — I heard this from others.)
- **wollen** = *the subject themselves* claims this about themselves, often with a skeptical
  undertone from the speaker: *Er will das gewusst haben.* (He claims to have known that — but
  I'm not sure I believe him.)

A useful test: if you can insert "angeblich" (allegedly) and the meaning holds, you are likely
looking at subjective sollen or wollen.

## können in the negative: disbelief

Subjective *können* in a negative sentence expresses that something seems impossible or hard to
believe:

> **Das kann nicht wahr sein.** (That can't be true.)
> **Das kann doch nicht sein Ernst sein!** (He can't be serious!)

## Speculating about the past: the perfect infinitive

To speculate about something that already happened, keep the modal verb in the **present**
tense and turn the main verb into a **perfect infinitive**: Partizip II + haben/sein.

> **Er muss den Zug verpasst haben.** (He must have missed the train.)
> **Sie dürfte das Meeting vergessen haben.** (She probably forgot the meeting.)
> **Er will das nicht gewusst haben.** (He claims not to have known that.)

Notice the modal verb itself never shifts to a past tense (~~er musste den Zug verpasst
haben~~ is wrong) — only the embedded infinitive becomes perfect.

## Why this matters at C1

Native speakers use subjective modals constantly to hedge, speculate, and attribute claims
without committing to their truth — a crucial skill for nuanced writing, reporting, and everyday
conversation about things you cannot personally verify.
