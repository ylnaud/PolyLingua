Textbook German gets you understood. Idioms — **Redewendungen** — get you laughed with
instead of at, because they are the phrases native speakers actually reach for at the
coffee machine, in the group chat, and on the evening news. Translating them literally
almost never works, because each one is really a small, frozen picture: a sack that
might hold a cat, thumbs pressed instead of fingers crossed, a broom somebody is
willing to eat.

## Why idioms resist translation

Every language encodes emotion, certainty, and judgment in different physical images.
English speakers cross their fingers; Germans press their thumbs. English speakers
eat their hat when proven wrong; Germans eat a broom. The grammar is often simple —
these are short, common sentences — but the *meaning lives entirely in the image*, so
a word-for-word translation either means nothing or means something unintentionally
strange. Learning idioms is really learning to see the world the way German phrases
see it.

## The eyes and the obvious: Tomaten auf den Augen haben

When a friend walks right past the salt on the table while searching for it, you say:
**"Du hast wohl Tomaten auf den Augen!"** — you clearly have tomatoes on your eyes.
It is gently teasing, never harsh, and it is one of the most common idioms for
"you're not seeing what's right in front of you."

## Wishing luck with your thumbs

Forget crossed fingers. In German-speaking countries, you make a loose fist and press
your thumb into it — **die Daumen drücken**. Before an exam, a job interview, or a
big match, you'll hear **"Ich drücke dir die Daumen!"** — I'm rooting for you. If you
cross your fingers instead while speaking German, native listeners will understand
the sentiment but notice the gesture is "wrong" for the phrase.

## Indifference, wrapped in sausage

**"Das ist mir Wurst"** (also heard as the more colorful *Das ist mir Wurscht* in the
south) means "I genuinely don't care, it's all the same to me." The logic: one
sausage looks and tastes roughly like another, so which one you get doesn't matter.
It is casual and mildly informal — reach for it with friends, not in a job interview.

## Buying the cat in the sack

**"Die Katze im Sack kaufen"** — to buy the cat in the sack — describes committing to
something without checking it first: a used car bought unseen, an apartment rented
from photos alone. The image dates back to medieval markets, where a dishonest seller
might swap a valuable piglet for a worthless cat inside a closed sack, hoping the
buyer wouldn't look until it was too late.

## Total confusion: nur Bahnhof verstehen

**"Ich verstehe nur Bahnhof"** — I only understand train station — means you have
completely lost the thread of a conversation; none of it is registering. Nobody
fully agrees on the origin (one popular story ties it to WWI soldiers longing to hear
only the word "Bahnhof," meaning they were headed home, and tuning everything else
out), but every German speaker recognizes the phrase instantly.

## Staking your certainty on a broom

When Germans want to say "I am completely certain, and if I'm wrong I'll eat
something absurd," English reaches for a hat — German reaches for a broom:
**"Ich fresse einen Besen, wenn das stimmt!"** ("Fressen" here is the verb normally
used for animals eating, which adds a note of self-mocking exaggeration to the
claim.)

## How to actually learn these

Don't memorize idioms as vocabulary lists — memorize the *image* attached to each
one, and notice them when native speakers use them in shows, podcasts, or
conversation. The picture is what makes the phrase stick, and it's also what makes
it fun: German idioms are full of tomatoes, brooms, sausages, and cats in sacks, and
once you start noticing them, you'll hear them everywhere.
