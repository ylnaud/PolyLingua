**Sprichwörter** — proverbs — are complete little sentences of inherited wisdom, and
they are different from the idioms (Redewendungen) in the previous lesson in one key
way: a proverb usually stands alone as a full statement of advice, while an idiom is
a phrase you plug into a sentence you're already building. Both are essential for
sounding like a native speaker, but proverbs carry a particular weight — they're
quoted by grandparents, printed on kitchen towels, and dropped into conversation as
gentle, time-tested advice.

## Like father, like son — with an apple tree

**"Der Apfel fällt nicht weit vom Stamm"** — the apple doesn't fall far from the
trunk — is said the moment someone notices a child sharing a parent's temperament,
talent, or flaw. It works for compliments ("she's just as musical as her mother — the
apple doesn't fall far from the tree") and for exasperated observations ("just as
stubborn as his father...") equally well.

## Gold in the mouth of the morning

**"Morgenstund hat Gold im Mund"** rhymes on purpose — *Morgenstund* and *Mund* —
which is exactly why its grammar looks a little frozen in time: modern German would
normally add an *-e* to *Mund* in this dative construction, but the proverb keeps the
older, shorter form because changing it would break the rhyme. The meaning: early
hours are especially valuable, so get up and start your day. In practice, it's often
said half-teasingly to someone struggling to leave bed, the way "the early bird
catches the worm" gets deployed in English.

## Patience for problems that haven't arrived yet

**"Kommt Zeit, kommt Rat"** — literally "time comes, advice comes" — is the proverb
for premature worry. It reassures that a solution or clarity will present itself once
the situation actually develops, so there's no need to agonize over a problem before
it's fully formed. It pairs naturally with "we'll cross that bridge when we come to
it," though the German original leans more toward quiet trust than English's more
active metaphor.

## Don't celebrate too soon

**"Man soll den Tag nicht vor dem Abend loben"** — one should not praise the day
before the evening — is the German instinct against premature victory laps. A deal
that seems done, a plan that seems to be working, a trip that's gone smoothly so
far: this proverb is the reminder that anything can still go wrong before it's
truly over.

## Following through on your own commitments

**"Wer A sagt, muss auch B sagen"** points at the moment someone wants to back out
halfway through something they started. Having said "A" — made the first
commitment, taken the first step — the proverb insists you also have to say "B":
finish what you started, live with the consequences of the choice you already made.

## Proverbs as linguistic fossils

Notice how proverbs often sound slightly older or stiffer than everyday spoken
German — that's not your imagination. Because proverbs get passed down nearly
word-for-word across generations, they preserve grammatical forms (like the dative
*Mund* above) that the rest of the language has since simplified or dropped.
Linguists call this fossilization, and it happens in every language — English still
says "so be it" and "woe is me," constructions nobody would build from scratch in
modern speech. Learning a handful of German Sprichwörter, then, is a little bit like
learning a handful of small, quotable time capsules.
