Standard German (Hochdeutsch) is what textbooks teach and what news anchors in
Germany speak — but it is far from the only German that tens of millions of native
speakers use every day. At C2 level, real comprehension means being able to
recognize **Austrian German** and **Swiss German** when you meet them, instead of
being thrown by unfamiliar words and sounds you were never taught.

## Austria: its own standard, with protected words

Austrian German isn't a dialect layered on top of Standard German — it's a
recognized national variety with its own vocabulary for everyday items:
**Paradeiser** instead of *Tomate* (tomato), **Erdäpfel** instead of *Kartoffel*
(potato), **Marille** instead of *Aprikose* (apricot). Some of these words are
formally protected: when Austria joined the EU, a list of Austrian food terms was
written into the accession treaty specifically so Austrian German words wouldn't be
quietly replaced by the German-German equivalents on packaging and menus.

The everyday greeting **"Grüß Gott"** (literally "greet God," i.e. "may God greet
you") is standard, non-religious small talk in Austria and Catholic Southern Germany
— much like "goodbye" no longer registers as a literal blessing to most English
speakers who say it.

## Switzerland: a whole family of dialects, and a linguistic split

**Schweizerdeutsch** is not one dialect — it's an umbrella term for a family of
Alemannic dialects (Zurich German, Bernese German, Baseldeutsch, and more) that can
differ noticeably from each other and, more sharply, from Standard German. Swiss
German is used constantly in daily spoken life: at home, at work, on the street, even
in most spoken media.

Written German — and a more formal spoken **Schweizer Hochdeutsch**, still carrying a
distinct Swiss accent and cadence — is reserved for writing, official documents,
school instruction, and formal broadcasts like the evening news. This everyday split
between a spoken home variety and a formal written/spoken standard is called
**Diglossie** (diglossia), and it's why Swiss shows aimed at German audiences often
carry German subtitles: the dialect gap is real enough that Standard-German-only
speakers can genuinely struggle.

**"Grüezi"** is the greeting most learners encounter first — a friendly, widely
recognized Swiss German hello that instantly signals you're listening to Swiss
speech rather than German or Austrian.

## Why this matters at C2

A textbook fluent in Hochdeutsch alone can still be caught off guard by an Austrian
menu, a Swiss radio interview, or a colleague from Vienna or Zurich using vocabulary
that was simply never taught. These aren't rare curiosities — Austria and
German-speaking Switzerland together represent well over ten million native speakers
for whom these forms *are* the everyday norm, not an exception to it. Recognizing
**Grüezi** versus **Grüß Gott** versus a plain **Hallo**, or **Paradeiser** versus
**Tomate**, is a fast, practical way to place where a German speaker is likely from —
and to avoid the confusion of assuming Standard German is the only "real" German.

## A practical listening tip

You don't need to learn to *speak* Austrian or Swiss German fluently to benefit from
this lesson — the real C2 skill is passive recognition. Notice the vocabulary swaps,
notice the greetings, and notice that Swiss Hochdeutsch on the news still sounds
different from a German or Austrian newscaster, even though the grammar on the page
would look identical. That recognition is what separates "I learned German" from
"I understand German as it's actually spoken across three countries."
