Two hours have passed since you reported the heating problem, and nothing has changed.
You go back to reception — this time more firmly, but still politely. Here, the grammar
tool that carries the firmness is different from the "if" clauses of lesson 1: it's
**would**, used to state an expectation.

## The 5 chunks for this situation

| English                                                          | Español                                                           |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| I already mentioned this two hours ago, but nothing has changed. | Ya lo mencioné hace dos horas, pero nada ha cambiado.             |
| I really can't stay here like this.                              | Realmente no puedo quedarme aquí así.                             |
| I'd expect you to give me a discount on the room.                | Esperaría que me diera un descuento en la habitación.             |
| What would you suggest as a solution?                            | ¿Qué sugeriría como solución?                                     |
| If the problem can't be fixed, I'll have to change hotels.       | Si el problema no se puede resolver, tendré que cambiar de hotel. |

## The full conversation

> **Guest:** I already mentioned this two hours ago, but nothing has changed.
> **Receptionist:** I'm truly sorry.
> **Guest:** I'd expect you to give me a discount on the room.
> **Receptionist:** I understand. What would you suggest as a solution?
> **Guest:** If the problem can't be fixed, I'll have to change hotels.

## Work it out: "would give", not a short special form

Compare **"I'd expect you to give me..."** ("would" + infinitive) with the "if + past
simple" pattern from lesson 1 ("if we had a free room"). Both use a form of hypothetical
distance to sound polite, but here there's no "if" clause at all — just "would" attached
directly to an expectation.

Ask yourself: why doesn't English have a shorter, special form of "give" the way some
languages have distinct subjunctive endings? Think about how "would" behaves the same
way no matter which verb follows it (would give, would suggest, would help...).

That's the pattern: **English uses "would" + bare infinitive as its all-purpose
politeness/hypothetical marker**, for almost any verb, instead of changing the verb's own
form. It's simpler than languages with a separate subjunctive conjugation — one tool,
"would", does the job everywhere.

Notice also the last line: **"If the problem can't be fixed, I'll have to change
hotels"** switches to the **first conditional** (if + present, will + infinitive) — a
real, open possibility, not the hypothetical distance of "if we had a room" from lesson 1.

## Practise out loud (shadowing)

Read the conversation three times:

1. **Slowly**, isolating "would" every time — notice it never changes form, no matter the
   verb that follows.
2. **At natural speed**, with a firm but calm tone — the voice drops slightly and stays
   serious on "I really can't stay here like this," without sounding aggressive.
3. **Recording yourself**, chaining the five lines as a real follow-up complaint. Also
   write a short follow-up message asking for a solution to a different problem, reusing
   at least two chunks above, and save it with the recording.
